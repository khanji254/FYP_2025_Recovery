\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This chapter presents the \textit{what, why, and how} of the N4 recovery-system design process. In line with the interim-report guidelines, this chapter contains design considerations, calculations, circuit and CAD development workflow, and implementation planning for each subsystem.

\subsection{Design Approach and Subsystem Decomposition}

\subsection{Methodology structure}

The N4 recovery work follows an iterative design–build–test cycle. For clarity and traceability this chapter separates the lifecycle into two orthogonal views: (A) the project phases (linear progression through development and verification) and (B) the implementation modules (concurrent subsystem workstreams).

\paragraph{Project phases}
1. Requirements definition — derive subsystem requirements from mission constraints and safety limits.
2. Concept generation — produce candidate mechanical, electrical and software solutions.
3. Concept selection — evaluate candidates against performance, manufacturability and safety.
4. Design and build — produce CAD, schematics and prototype hardware; capture PCB/EAGLE/EasyEDA sources.
5. Firmware implementation — develop modular ESP32 firmware and inter-module interfaces.
6. Validation and testing — staged bench and system-level tests, logging, and pre‑flight checks.

\paragraph{Implementation modules}
These workstreams run in parallel across the phases above:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Telemetry and antenna strategy (XBee, beacon, ground-station),
  \item Pyrotechnic deployment and ignition control (PWM, MOSFET drive),
  \item Power and feedback instrumentation (ADS1115 monitoring, continuity sensing),
  \item Mechanical containment and avionics support structure (holder, battery pack),
  \item Embedded software and communication control (FSM, CommunicationManager).
\end{enumerate}

This structure keeps the chapter descriptive (``what we did and why'') rather than prescriptive, and maps directly to the deliverables referenced in Chapter~4 and the Appendices
\subsection{Telemetry Module Design Methodology}

\subsubsection{Telemetry Module Scope}

The telemetry module covers flight-to-ground data transmission hardware, antenna strategy, communication reliability constraints, and integration interfaces to the ESP32 flight computer firmware \cite{xbee_manual, n4_xbee_integration, n4_comm_architecture}.

\subsubsection{Telemetry Design Considerations}

Design considerations for telemetry selection are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Minimum operational range of at least 5~km line-of-sight.
    \item Robust packet delivery with low drop rate in dynamic flight attitude.
    \item Simple and deterministic integration with ESP32 UART telemetry output.
    \item Field-deployable ground-station setup without complex infrastructure.
    \item Compatibility with future communication redundancy (beacon/MQTT fallback).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Chosen Telemetry Design and Justification}

The selected telemetry hardware is the \textbf{Digi XBee-PRO 900HP} operating in transparent UART mode because it provides strong range performance, straightforward ESP32 integration, and robust field operation with minimal protocol complexity \cite{xbee_manual, n4_xbee_integration, n4_comm_architecture}.

\subsubsection{Communication Mode Architecture and the Beacon Protocol}

The N4 flight computer implements three independent communication channels managed by a software CommunicationManager \cite{n4_comm_architecture}: (1)~MQTT over WiFi for close-range pad operations, (2)~the 900~MHz XBee link for primary long-range recovery telemetry, and (3)~a \textit{beacon protocol} currently being researched as a redundant fallback channel.

\textbf{What is the Beacon protocol?} A beacon is a raw 802.11 management frame injected directly into the IEEE~802.11 (2.4~GHz WiFi) radio medium without establishing a conventional network association. The rocket does not connect to any access point; instead, the ESP32 radio injects custom management frames --- each carrying the telemetry CSV payload in a vendor-specific information element --- directly into the air at a user-defined interval. A ground-station ESP32 running in promiscuous (\textit{sniffer}) mode captures all frames that match the payload magic number regardless of association state, and extracts the RSSI directly from the hardware receive descriptor \cite{n4_beacon_configuration}. The return path (ground to rocket) uses ESP-NOW unicast packets, which provide confirmed delivery without requiring a broker or access point.

The beacon system has been field-tested to 4~km line-of-sight using a 2.4~GHz duck antenna and inline signal amplifier on the rocket side, and a directional patch antenna with amplifier at the ground station \cite{n4_beacon_configuration}. It is employed as the \textbf{redundant research channel} --- the XBee 900~MHz link remains the primary mission telemetry path. The CommunicationManager supports automatic fallback from MQTT to beacon when WiFi connectivity is lost, and allows runtime switching between all three modes via serial, MQTT, or ESP-NOW commands \cite{n4_comm_architecture}. The telemetry architecture is shown in Appendix~\ref{app:moved_figures} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_method_xbee_system}).

The selected baseline antenna pair for current implementation is the 900~MHz duck dipole. Its feasibility is checked with the first-order link budget. Free-space path loss (FSPL) models the signal attenuation over distance in the absence of obstacles \cite{pozar_microwave,xbee_manual}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FSPL}\,(\mathrm{dB}) = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) - 147.55
\label{eq:fspl}
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the link distance in metres, $f$ is the carrier frequency in hertz, and 147.55 is the SI-unit normalisation constant derived from the $\lambda/(4\pi R)$ free-space propagation factor \cite{pozar_microwave}.

At the N4 target range of $d = 5{,}000$~m and centre frequency of $f = 915\times10^6$~Hz, the free-space path loss is 105.66~dB, which is within the expected range for 900~MHz ISM-band communications at this distance \cite{xbee_manual}.
Received power at the ground station is obtained from the link-budget equation \cite{pozar_microwave,xbee_manual}:
\begin{equation}
P_{rx}=P_{tx}+G_{tx}+G_{rx}-\mathrm{FSPL}
\label{eq:prx}
\end{equation}

where $P_{tx}$ is the transmit power in dBm, $G_{tx}$ and $G_{rx}$ are the transmit and receive antenna gains in dBi, and FSPL is the free-space path loss calculated above (dB). The XBee-PRO 900HP rated output is $P_{tx}=+24$~dBm \cite{xbee_manual}, and both ends use duck dipole antennas with $G_{tx}=G_{rx}=2$~dBi. From equation~\ref{eq:prx} the received power at the ground station, $P_{rx}$, was obtained as $-77.66$~dBm.

Link margin quantifies the safety buffer above the minimum detectable signal threshold \cite{pozar_microwave}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Margin}=P_{rx}-P_{sens}
\label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}

where $P_{sens}$ is the XBee-PRO 900HP rated receiver sensitivity ($-110$~dBm at 10\% packet-error rate \cite{xbee_manual}). Using the values above gives a link margin of $32.34$~dB, which leaves enough headroom for atmospheric loss, multipath fading, and small misalignments. The antenna pattern discussion is continued in Appendix~\ref{app:moved_figures} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_dipole_wave_profile}).

To mitigate dipole null effects at the ground segment, a \textbf{Bi-Quad antenna is being considered as a potential base-station alternative} for improved directional gain and better coverage across critical tracking sectors \cite{arrl_antenna_book, pozar_microwave}.

\subsection{Electrical Module with Deployment Submodule Methodology}

\subsubsection{Electrical + Deployment Module Scope}

This module covers onboard signal interfacing, telemetry electrical routing, power/continuity monitoring, and pyrotechnic actuation electronics. It is paired with the deployment submodule because ignition-drive design depends directly on electrical interface limits and power budget constraints.

\subsubsection{Design Considerations}

Key electrical/deployment design considerations are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The flight computer has limited available pins, requiring protocol-level optimization between UART and I$^2$C paths.
    \item Telemetry radio (XBee) requires hardware serial, with remapped RX/TX pins on ESP32 to preserve other interfaces.
    \item Power monitoring and chute-state sensing require additional channels beyond native MCU pin allocation, motivating ADC expansion over I$^2$C.
    \item The electronics stack must fit inside the 95~mm avionics bay and align with holder mounting geometry and rod clearances.
    \item The deployment channel is validated against a 16.8~V fully charged 4S LiPo input and a 14~V minimum arm threshold so the sensing and inhibit logic cover both the worst-case supply peak and the lowest safe pre-fire condition.
    \item Pyro switching must be compatible with 3.3~V logic drive and sustain current pulses without excessive heating.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Power-Monitoring Calculations (ADS1115)}

This subsection describes the ADS1115 battery-monitoring and chute-state sensing design. The divider is sized for a 4S LiPo upper limit of 16.8~V and a 14~V minimum arm threshold so the monitoring and inhibit logic cover the full safe operating window.
The monitoring path uses a 4.7~k$\Omega$/1.1~k$\Omega$ voltage divider into the ADS1115 \cite{ads1115_datasheet}. A voltage divider scales an input voltage proportionally using two series resistors \cite{voltage_divider_theory}:
\begin{equation}
V_{ADC}=V_{in}\cdot\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}=V_{in}\cdot\frac{1.1}{4.7+1.1}
\label{eq:vadc_divider}
\end{equation}

where $V_{in}$ is the battery bus voltage to be measured, $R_1=4.7$~k$\Omega$ is the upper divider resistor, $R_2=1.1$~k$\Omega$ is the lower resistor, and $V_{ADC}$ is the scaled output voltage presented to the ADC pin.

For $V_{in}=16.8$~V:
\begin{equation}
V_{ADC,16.8}=16.8\cdot\frac{1.1}{4.7+1.1}\approx 3.186\,\mathrm{V}
\label{eq:vadc168}
\end{equation}

For $V_{in}=14$~V:
\begin{equation}
V_{ADC,14}=14\cdot\frac{1.1}{4.7+1.1}\approx 2.655\,\mathrm{V}
\label{eq:vadc14}
\end{equation}

The ADS1115 is configured at a $\pm4.096$~V full-scale range (FSR) with 16-bit resolution (32{,}768 counts over the positive half-range) \cite{ads1115_datasheet}. The corresponding digital code is:
\begin{equation}
N=\frac{V_{ADC}}{4.096}\times32{,}767
\label{eq:adc_counts}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the 16-bit ADC output count and 32{,}767 is the maximum positive code ($2^{15}-1$). For the two design points:
\begin{equation}
N_{16.8}\approx 25{,}487 \quad (\text{at }V_{in}=16.8\,\mathrm{V}),\qquad N_{14}\approx 21{,}242 \quad (\text{at }V_{in}=14\,\mathrm{V})
\label{eq:adc_counts_values}
\end{equation}

These ranges provide clear monitoring margin below ADC full-scale while preserving sensitivity to deployment-state transitions.

\subsubsection{Deployment PWM and MOSFET Selection}

The pyro channels are driven through PWM-controlled MOSFET stages to avoid nichrome burnout and improve reusability across repeated ground tests \cite{pwm_heating, barr_pwm, n4_pyro_control, n4_pwm_config}.

\textbf{(a) Nichrome wire element characterisation:}

The igniter elements use 26~AWG nichrome resistance wire with a linear resistance of approximately 8.5~$\Omega$/m \cite{nichrome_ignition}. For a representative bridge-wire element of length $l_{e}=0.10$~m (10~cm), the igniter element resistance is:
\begin{equation}
R_{elem}=\rho_{NiCr}\cdot l_{e}
\label{eq:relem_formula}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_{NiCr}$ is the linear resistance per unit length ($\Omega$/m) and $l_{e}$ is the active element length (m). Substituting values:
\begin{equation}
R_{elem}=8.5\times0.10=0.85\,\Omega
\label{eq:relem}
\end{equation}

At a target drive voltage $V_{target}=6.0$~V, steady-state current in the element is given by Ohm's Law \cite{voltage_divider_theory}:
\begin{equation}
I_{elem}=\frac{V_{target}}{R_{elem}}
\label{eq:ielem_formula}
\end{equation}

Substituting:
\begin{equation}
I_{elem}=\frac{6.0}{0.85}\approx7.06\,\mathrm{A}
\label{eq:ielem}
\end{equation}

Corresponding instantaneous resistive power dissipation:
\begin{equation}
P_{wire}=\frac{V_{target}^{2}}{R_{elem}}
\label{eq:pwire_formula}
\end{equation}

Substituting:
\begin{equation}
P_{wire}=\frac{6.0^{2}}{0.85}\approx42.4\,\mathrm{W}
\label{eq:pwire}
\end{equation}

Using PWM to limit the effective voltage below the ignition threshold until arm state is confirmed prevents unintended heating on the bench. Bench characterisation with 26~AWG elements showed that at minimum PWM duty cycle the wire withstood approximately 30~seconds of sustained energisation without failure, compared to burnout within 2~seconds at maximum (DC-equivalent) duty. Pyrotechnic ignition and pop tests have not yet been conducted and are scheduled for the next project phase.

\textbf{(b) PWM duty-cycle model:}

PWM controls the average voltage seen by the nichrome wire by rapidly switching the MOSFET on and off. The average output voltage is proportional to the duty cycle \cite{barr_pwm,n4_pyro_control}:
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{V_{target}}{V_{supply}}
\label{eq:pwm_duty}
\end{equation}

where $D$ is the dimensionless duty cycle (0 to 1), $V_{target}$ is the desired average element voltage, and $V_{supply}$ is the LiPo supply voltage. For the nominal case $V_{target}=6.0$~V and $V_{supply}=15.0$~V:
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{6.0}{15.0} = 0.40 \quad (\text{i.e.\ }40\%\text{ duty cycle})
\label{eq:pwm_duty_value}
\end{equation}

\textbf{(c) IRLZ44N vs IRF-type MOSFET choice:}

The selected switch is IRLZ44N (logic-level gate-drive compatible) rather than a standard IRF-series part, because the ESP32 gate output is 3.3~V and logic-level devices achieve low on-state resistance at reduced gate voltage \cite{irlz44n_datasheet,mosfet_switching}. Conduction loss in a MOSFET switch is \cite{mosfet_switching}:
\begin{equation}
P_{cond}=I^2 R_{DS(on)}
\label{eq:pcond_formula}
\end{equation}

where $I$ is the drain current and $R_{DS(on)}$ is the drain-to-source on-resistance. The IRLZ44N is rated $V_{DSS}=55$~V, $I_D=47$~A, with $R_{DS(on)}=22$~m$\Omega$ at $V_{GS}=5$~V \cite{irlz44n_datasheet}. For a representative $I=3$~A pulse through a logic-level switch ($R_{DS(on)}\approx0.022\,\Omega$):
\begin{equation}
P_{cond,IRLZ}=3^2\times0.022\approx0.20\,\mathrm{W}
\label{eq:pcond_irlz}
\end{equation}

For a standard non-logic-level part at low gate drive ($R_{DS(on)}\gtrsim0.05\,\Omega$):
\begin{equation}
P_{cond,IRF}\gtrsim3^2\times0.05\gtrsim0.45\,\mathrm{W}
\label{eq:pcond_irf}
\end{equation}

The lower dissipation and reliable 3.3~V gate compatibility motivate selecting IRLZ44N for the deployment channels.

\subsubsection{Additional Implementation Details}

To complete the electrical-deployment pairing, the design also includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hardware serial pin remapping for XBee RX/TX on ESP32 without blocking I$^2$C monitoring.
    \item Segregated low-voltage logic and high-energy pyro routing on PCB for reduced coupling.
    \item Pre-arm validation of battery/chute channels before enabling deployment PWM outputs.

\subsection{Structural (Mechanical) Module Design Methodology}

\subsubsection{Structural Module Scope}

The structural module defines the flight-computer holder, battery-carrier geometry, threaded-rod integration, mounting-hole allocation, and interface provision for the XBee extension board and battery management system (BMS). Beyond individual component retention, the holder functions as the primary mechanical bed that fastens the complete battery pack as a unified assembly and provides the upper-platform mounting surface for the XBee extension board and beacon antenna module.

\subsubsection{Structural Design Considerations}

The structural design constraints are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fit within the 95~mm avionics-bay envelope.
    \item Survive launch and deployment shock/vibration events (design baseline: up to 4g sustained/inertial loading, with higher short-duration pop-test transients).
    \item Package four 18650 cells in parallel while avoiding rod interference.
    \item Maintain two threaded rods at 50~mm spacing and approximately 6~mm offset from the rear holder reference edge.
    \item Raise edge battery seats relative to mid seats so outer cells clear rod paths.
    \item Allocate and distribute self-tapping screw locations for load sharing.
    \item Provide mounting provision above battery holders for XBee extension and BMS.
    \item Include threaded holes for brass columns used to mount the flight computer.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Structural Load Calculations}

\paragraph{(a) 18650 battery mass and weight}
Using a representative 18650 cell mass of $m_{cell}\approx0.047$~kg (typical of high-capacity 18650 datasheets \cite{panasonic_cgr18650da_datasheet}), total pack mass is:
\begin{equation}
m_{pack}=n_{cells}\cdot m_{cell}
\label{eq:mpack_formula}
\end{equation}

where $n_{cells}=4$ is the number of cells. Substituting:
\begin{equation}
m_{pack}=4\times0.047=0.188\,\mathrm{kg}
\label{eq:mpack}
\end{equation}

Static gravitational load from the four cells:
\begin{equation}
W_{pack}=m_{pack}\cdot g
\label{eq:wpack_formula}
\end{equation}

where $g=9.81$~m/s$^2$ is the standard gravitational acceleration. Substituting:
\begin{equation}
W_{pack}=0.188\times9.81\approx1.84\,\mathrm{N}
\label{eq:wpack}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(b) Launch inertial force (4g design case)}
The inertial load on the battery pack during launch is obtained from Newton's second law \cite{nakka_thrust,nakka_eqns}:
\begin{equation}
F_{inertial}=m_{pack}\cdot a_{launch}
\label{eq:flaunch_formula}
\end{equation}

where $a_{launch}$ is the peak launch acceleration. For a 4g design envelope:
\begin{equation}
a_{launch}=4g=4\times9.81=39.24\,\mathrm{m/s^2}
\label{eq:alaunch}
\end{equation}

Substituting:
\begin{equation}
F_{launch}=0.188\times39.24\approx7.38\,\mathrm{N}
\label{eq:flaunch}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(c) Pop-test pressure-driven force (188~kPa reference)}
Pressure force on a surface is \cite{n4_poptest_internal}:
\begin{equation}
F=P\cdot A
\label{eq:fpop_formula}
\end{equation}

where $P$ is the applied pressure (Pa) and $A$ is the projected area (m$^2$). The previously recorded pop-test overpressure reference is $P_{pop}=188$~kPa \cite{n4_poptest_internal}. The conservative projected area uses the full 95~mm bay section:
\begin{equation}
A_{95}=\pi\left(\frac{0.095}{2}\right)^2\approx7.09\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}
\label{eq:a95}
\end{equation}

Substituting:
\begin{equation}
F_{pop}=P_{pop}\cdot A_{95}=188{,}000\times7.09\times10^{-3}\approx1.33\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}
\label{eq:fpop}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(d) Impulse estimate for pop transient}
Impulse is the product of force and the duration over which it acts:
\begin{equation}
J=F_{pop}\cdot\Delta t
\label{eq:impulse_pop}
\end{equation}

where $J$ is impulse (N$\cdot$s) and $\Delta t$ is the pressure-pulse duration. For a representative short pressure-pulse duration $\Delta t=10$~ms:
\begin{equation}
J\approx1{,}330\times0.01\approx13.3\,\mathrm{N\cdot s}
\label{eq:impulse_pop_value}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(e) Load distribution through fasteners and supports}
For distributed fastening with $n$ screw/support points, first-order load per point under a total applied force $F$ is:
\begin{equation}
F_{point}=\frac{F}{n}
\label{eq:fpoint}
\end{equation}

where $n$ is the number of fastener points. Under $F_{pop}\approx1{,}330$~N: 4 points carry $\sim332$~N each; 8 points carry $\sim166$~N each. This demonstrates why distributed fastening reduces local stress concentration and improves structural reliability.

\subsection{Embedded Software Methodology}

\subsubsection{Software Module Scope}

The software module governs all firmware executing on the ESP32 flight computer, covering three implementation areas: (i)~telemetry and XBee communication management, (ii)~pyrotechnic deployment state-machine control via PWM, and (iii)~power and continuity monitoring integration. Area~(iii) is in active development and has not yet been validated against live hardware at the time of this report.

\subsubsection{Software Design Considerations}

Three core problems drive the software design:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{XBee Integration:} The XBee-PRO 900HP must stream telemetry reliably at a deterministic data rate using hardware serial. Interface selection (SPI vs UART), data rate constraints, and pre-flight radio configuration via XCTU must all be resolved before firmware integration.
    \item \textbf{Pyrotechnic Deployment via PWM:} The deployment state machine must safely arm, fire, and confirm chute deployment events using PWM-gated MOSFET drive without false actuation or premature triggering under vibration or electromagnetic interference.
    \item \textbf{Power Monitoring Integration:} Real-time battery voltage and chute-channel continuity feedback from the ADS1115 must be incorporated into pre-flight arm logic and appended to the outbound telemetry packet. This integration is not yet complete in the current firmware build.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{XBee Communication Interface: SPI vs UART}

The XBee-PRO 900HP hardware exposes only a UART serial interface for transparent-mode data transfer; it does not offer SPI or I\textsuperscript{2}C bus modes \cite{xbee_manual}. SPI is therefore inapplicable, and the design uses the ESP32 hardware UART peripheral with remapped RX/TX GPIO pins, preserving all other peripheral assignments on the flight computer \cite{esp32_datasheet}.

\subsubsection{Radio Pre-Flight Configuration via XCTU}

XCTU is Digi's official configuration and test utility for XBee modules. The N4 pre-flight procedure programs both flight and ground-station units via XCTU before each integration cycle \cite{xbee_manual}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Network ID (ID):} Set identically on both units (e.g., \texttt{7FFF}) to isolate the N4 network from other XBee traffic in the field.
    \item \textbf{Baud Rate (BD):} Set to 115200 on both units to match firmware UART initialization.
    \item \textbf{Channel Mask:} 900~MHz ISM band frequency-hopping profile configured to minimize ambient interference.
    \item \textbf{Transmit Power (PL):} Maximum (+24~dBm) for full downlink link-margin.
    \item \textbf{API Mode (AP):} Set to 0 (transparent mode) so the module passes raw bytes directly without packet-framing overhead, which suits the simple CSV/binary telemetry stream.
    \item \textbf{Sleep Mode (SM):} Disabled; module remains active continuously during all flight phases.
\end{enumerate}

After configuration, a loopback range test confirms bidirectional packet delivery before avionics bay assembly. Module profiles are saved in XCTU for rapid re-flashing if hardware replacement is required before launch.

\subsubsection{Power Monitoring Software Integration (In Progress)}

Battery voltage and chute-channel continuity are read from the ADS1115 over I\textsuperscript{2}C and back-calculated to real-world voltage using the inverse of the hardware voltage divider \cite{ads1115_datasheet}:
\begin{equation}
V_{battery} = V_{ADC} \times \frac{R_1+R_2}{R_2} = V_{ADC} \times \frac{57.0}{10.0}
\label{eq:vbatt_sw}
\end{equation}

where $V_{ADC}$ is the raw ADC reading in volts, and the factor 57/10 is the reciprocal of the hardware divider ratio \cite{voltage_divider_theory}.

The monitoring flow is presented in Appendix~\ref{app:moved_figures} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_sw_power_flow}).

The reconstructed voltage is appended to the telemetry packet and compared against the inhibit threshold. If the value falls below \texttt{LAUNCH\_INHIBIT\_V}, the deployment arm flag is cleared and a ``\texttt{PWR INHIBIT}'' status byte is broadcast over XBee to the ground station before launch \cite{n4_pyro_control}. The hardware concept for this monitor is shown in Appendix~\ref{app:moved_figures} (Figure~\ref{fig:app_sw_power_concept}).

\textbf{Current integration status:} The hardware design and Proteus simulation of the ADS1115 monitoring circuit are complete (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, Electrical Module). Firmware I\textsuperscript{2}C polling of ADS1115 channels and the voltage reconstruction function are coded but have not yet been validated against the assembled PCB. Integration testing is planned as the next milestone before launch readiness review.

\subsection{Validation and Test Method}

Testing is staged from component to full-system level:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Component level:} PWM-output verification, continuity checks, and ADC calibration.
    \item \textbf{Subsystem level:} telemetry range trials, ignition repeatability tests, and enclosure leak/charring checks.
    \item \textbf{System level:} integrated ground rehearsal with full communication stack and deployment command-path validation.
\end{itemize}
