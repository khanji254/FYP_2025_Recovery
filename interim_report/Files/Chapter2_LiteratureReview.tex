\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:review}

The global coffee industry plays a critical role in the economies of many developing
countries, including Kenya, where it provides livelihoods to millions of smallholder
farmers. Coffee processing involves several critical stages, among which the sorting
of coffee berries determines final product quality and market value. The presence of
unripe, overripe, or defective berries significantly degrades cup quality, leading to
price penalties and rejection by premium buyers \cite{brosnan2004,blasco2003}.

Automated sorting systems based on machine vision offer a path to consistent,
high-throughput grading \cite{li2022,kamilaris2018}. A previous fifth-year project at this
institution developed an image-based coffee berry sorting machine that demonstrated the
feasibility of automated sorting but revealed significant technical gaps in real-time
performance \cite{mugo2017}. This chapter documents that prior system in detail,
surveys the relevant literature across mechanical handling, vision hardware, deep
learning models, and embedded architectures, and identifies the specific gaps
addressed by this work.

\subsection{Previous Works}
\label{subsec:ref_project}

The foundation of this work is a fifth-year mechatronics engineering project
(FYP~22-22) at JKUAT that designed and built an automated coffee berry sorting
machine \cite{mugo2017}. That project established the core mechanical and electronic
architecture on which the current work builds, and its documented performance
shortcomings directly motivate the improvements proposed here.

\subsubsection{System Architecture of the Reference Machine}

The reference system integrated four main subsystems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mechanical handling:} A vibrating eccentric-motor tray singulated
        berries from a hopper onto an inclined chute. Gravity and vibration aligned
        berries into a near-single-file stream before the imaging zone.
  \item \textbf{Image acquisition:} A Raspberry Pi Camera Module V2 (rolling-shutter,
        8\,MP Sony IMX219) mounted above the chute captured frames at fixed intervals.
  \item \textbf{Classification model:} The reference project initially deployed a
        MobileNetV2-based model \cite{sandler2018,howard2017} for per-frame ripeness
        classification, exporting it as a TensorFlow Lite INT8 quantised file. In a
        subsequent revision, the team retrained using \textbf{YOLOv11n}
        \cite{ultralytics2024} to gain bounding-box localisation per berry. Despite
        the model upgrade to YOLOv11n, the pipeline remained serialised on a single
        Raspberry Pi~3B+, so the inference-latency bottleneck persisted.
  \item \textbf{Actuation:} Compressed-air solenoid jets, triggered through the
        Pi's GPIO pins via PN2222A BJT drivers, deflected reject-class berries into
        separate chutes; accepted berries continued to the accept bin under gravity.
\end{enumerate}

The mechanical chute and vibrating feeder proved robust and reliable in field
testing \cite{mugo2017}. This project retains that mechanical subsystem unchanged,
focusing improvements exclusively on the electronics, vision pipeline, and
software layers.

\subsubsection{Identified Limitations of the Reference Machine}

Post-build evaluation of the reference machine identified three critical performance
bottlenecks \cite{mugo2017}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Motion blur:} The rolling-shutter sensor read out rows sequentially
        over a $\sim$30\,ms window. At operating chute speeds of
        0.4--0.6\,m\,s$^{-1}$, berries translated 12--18\,mm per frame, producing
        visible smearing that degraded classification confidence, particularly at
        the semi-ripe/ripe colour boundary \cite{ramos2017}.
  \item \textbf{Processing latency:} Running YOLOv11n inference, GPIO triggering,
        and camera I/O on the Raspberry Pi~3B+ in a serialised single-threaded
        pipeline produced end-to-end latency of 280--400\,ms per berry, limiting
        practical throughput to approximately 35 berries per minute---far below the
        commercial target of $>$150 per minute \cite{mugo2017}.
  \item \textbf{Fine-grained class confusion:} The four-class model (unripe,
        semi-ripe, ripe, overripe) struggled with the visually similar semi-ripe and
        ripe classes. This confusion produced mis-ejections: commercially acceptable
        red berries were rejected and semi-ripe berries accepted into the output
        stream, reducing economic yield \cite{kumar2010,blasco2003}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Challenges of Manual and Early Automated Sorting}

\textbf{1. Labour Intensity and Fatigue:} Prolonged visual inspection leads to fatigue,
reducing sorting accuracy and consistency \cite{brosnan2004}. Fatigued workers are
more likely to misclassify berries, allowing defective berries to pass or rejecting
acceptable ones.

\textbf{2. Human Error and Subjectivity:} Significant variability in sorting outcomes
has been observed across different shifts, even when standardised grading guidelines
were provided \cite{blasco2003}. This subjectivity results in inconsistent quality between
batches.

\textbf{3. Limited Throughput:} Human sorting speed becomes a bottleneck during peak
harvest seasons \cite{kumar2010}. Automated systems address this but introduce the
challenges of motion blur, latency, and fine-grained class confusion documented above.

Vibrating feeder-chute systems are standard in agricultural sorting. The amplitude
and frequency of an eccentric motor are tuned to prevent bridging while maintaining
adequate throughput and berry singulation. Chute inclination angle and surface finish
further influence flow rate and separation. Literature confirms that this approach
produces reliably consistent berry spacing at throughputs of 100--300 berries per
minute in laboratory prototypes \cite{cubero2011}.

The air-jet deflection mechanism, which the reference system uses for separation,
operates on a detect-decide-deflect principle. Berries passing the imaging zone
trigger compressed-air jets timed to deflect reject-class berries into separate chutes
while accepted berries continue by gravity. Key advantages include minimal physical
contact and high speed; limitations include compressed-air infrastructure requirements
and tight timing constraints \cite{mahmood2019}.

\subsection{Camera Technology: Rolling Shutter vs.\ Global Shutter}
\label{subsec:shutter}

Conventional CMOS cameras read out pixel rows sequentially (rolling shutter). For
static scenes this is adequate, but when objects move faster than the sensor readout
time, each row captures a slightly different object position, producing a characteristic
shear distortion (jello effect) and edge smearing \cite{ramos2017}. In the reference
machine this was identified as the primary source of image degradation.

A \textbf{global-shutter} sensor exposes all pixels simultaneously, eliminating
motion-induced distortion entirely. Global-shutter CMOS sensors (e.g., Sony IMX296,
Omnivision OV9281) achieve effective shutter times of $<$1\,ms at full resolution.
At 0.5\,m\,s$^{-1}$ chute speed, a 1\,ms exposure limits berry translation to
0.5\,mm per frame---approximately 40$\times$ less motion blur than the reference
camera. This improvement is critical for reliable colour and texture classification
at the ripe/reject boundary.

For this project, the \textbf{Arducam OV9281 global-shutter camera module}
(Raspberry Pi CSI-compatible, 1\,MP, monochrome/colour variants) is proposed as
the replacement imaging sensor, providing freeze-frame clarity even at the target
operating chute speed.

\subsection{Deep Learning Object Detection for Sorting}

\subsubsection{CNN-Based Feature Extraction}

Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) automatically learn hierarchical features
from labelled image data, eliminating the need for hand-crafted descriptors \cite{lecun2015,he2016}. For
agricultural sorting, CNNs have consistently outperformed classical colour-histogram
and SVM-based approaches \cite{noble2006}, particularly under variable illumination
\cite{ramos2017}. Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2018} demonstrated that improved deep
convolutional architectures could identify disease symptoms on maize leaves with
high accuracy under field conditions, highlighting the robustness of learned features
across agricultural imaging tasks. A comprehensive review by Li et al.\ \cite{li2022}
and Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Bold\'u \cite{kamilaris2018} further confirm that deep
learning now dominates agricultural product quality grading, consistently
outperforming traditional machine-learning pipelines on colour, texture, and shape
discrimination tasks \cite{liakos2018,mohanty2016}.

\subsubsection{MobileNetV2 and its Limitations}

MobileNetV2 \cite{sandler2018,howard2017} uses inverted residual bottleneck blocks with
depthwise-separable convolutions to minimise parameter count for mobile deployment.
When quantised to INT8 and exported as TFLite, MobileNetV2 achieves inference times of
80--120\,ms on the Raspberry Pi~4. While fast enough in isolation, the reference
system could not overlap inference with the next frame capture, making the pipeline
strictly sequential and throughput-limited. Additionally, MobileNetV2 is a whole-image
classifier: it does not produce bounding boxes, so when multiple berries appear in one
frame it assigns a single class label to the entire image, causing mixed-berry
frames to be misclassified. These limitations motivated the reference team's migration
to YOLOv11n, but the serialised pipeline on the Pi~3B+ remained the throughput
bottleneck.

\subsubsection{YOLO Family: Real-Time Detection with Localisation}

YOLO (You Only Look Once) \cite{redmon2016} reformulated object detection as a
single regression problem, outputting bounding-box coordinates and class probabilities
in a single forward pass. Successive versions (YOLOv3 \cite{redmon2018},
YOLOv5 \cite{jocher2022}, YOLOv8 \cite{jocher2023}) progressively improved
small-object recall and speed \cite{reis2023}.

\subsubsection{YOLOv11 on Embedded Platforms}

YOLOv11, released by Ultralytics in late 2024, introduces architectural improvements
to the C2f backbone and neck that improve small-object detection and reduce parameter
count by $\approx$22\% compared to YOLOv8n \cite{ultralytics2024}. Critically, it
supports ONNX and TFLite export paths optimised for ARM Cortex-A72 (Raspberry
Pi~4) \cite{raspberrypi2019}, enabling inference at 25--40\,fps within a 1.5\,W
power envelope. Its bounding-box output makes it suitable for tracking individual
berries across frames \cite{bewley2016}, overcoming the whole-image classification
limitation of MobileNetV2.

\subsection{Binary (Accept/Reject) vs.\ Fine-Grained Classification}
\label{subsec:binary}

Industry coffee-sorting practice distinguishes primarily between \textit{acceptable}
(fully ripe, uniform red) and \textit{unacceptable} (green, yellow, overripe, defective)
berries \cite{blasco2003,cubero2011}. Semi-ripe berries---those in transitional
yellow-orange states---do not meet premium export grading standards and are
classified as rejects in all major Kenyan cooperative protocols \cite{sca2015}.

Fine-grained models that distinguish four or more intermediate classes amplify the
confusion between visually similar categories (e.g., semi-ripe and ripe), as documented
in the reference project \cite{mugo2017}. Binary classification, by contrast, reduces
the decision boundary to a single threshold in feature space: berries are labelled
\textbf{ACCEPT} (uniform deep-red, ripe) or \textbf{REJECT} (all other states:
green, yellow-orange, overripe, defective). This aligns with real-world sorting
practice and substantially reduces label ambiguity in the training set
\cite{kumar2010,gonzalez2018}.

This project adopts the binary scheme. The YOLOv11 model is trained and evaluated
on two classes only: \texttt{accept} and \texttt{reject}. Semi-ripe, overripe,
unripe, and visibly defective berries all map to the \texttt{reject} class.
Transfer learning from the YOLOv11n pretrained backbone \cite{jocher2023,tan2019}
allows the model to converge effectively on a dataset of only 2{,}000 images.

\subsection{Embedded Co-Processor Architectures}
\label{subsec:coprocessor}

Offloading time-critical real-time tasks to a dedicated microcontroller has been shown
to significantly improve throughput on single-board computer (SBC) vision systems
\cite{espressif2023,lin2017}. In multi-tier architectures, the SBC (Raspberry Pi) handles
computationally heavy tasks (frame capture, neural network inference, object tracking)
while a microcontroller (e.g., ESP32, STM32) is responsible for hard real-time
operations: solenoid pulse timing, PWM motor control, and relay switching
\cite{gonzalez2018}.

The \textbf{ESP32-WROOM-32} \cite{espressif2023} is a dual-core 240\,MHz
Xtensa LX6 microcontroller with native UART, SPI, I\textsuperscript{2}C, and 16
configurable PWM channels. Its deterministic ISR latency ($<$5\,\textmu{}s) and
hardware-timer-based solenoid firing eliminate the scheduling jitter inherent in
Linux-based Pi GPIO control. This is essential for accurate berry ejection timing
at conveyor speeds above 0.3\,m\,s$^{-1}$.

The proposed co-processing architecture assigns the Pi~4 to neural-network inference
and tracking, while the ESP32 exclusively handles solenoid, motor, and compressor
control. The two devices communicate over a UART link at 115\,200\,baud, with the
Pi transmitting short ASCII commands (e.g., \texttt{REJECT:2}) upon each ejection
decision.
