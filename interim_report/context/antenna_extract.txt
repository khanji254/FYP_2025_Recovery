1 Executive Summary
This document outlines the theoretical justification for achieving a telemetry link exceeding
5km range for a high-powered rocket flight. The system utilizes the Digi XBee-PRO
900HP (S3B) radio modules and standard 900MHz dipole “duck” antennas. Based on the
link budget calculations presented below, the specified hardware possesses sufficient power
margins to establish reliable communication at the target distance, provided that critical
operational alignments are maintained to mitigate antenna radiation pattern limitations
[1].

3 Theoretical Link Budget Calculation
To determine the viability of the link, we calculate the expected received signal strength
(Prx) using the Friis Transmission Equation [2].
3.1 Free Space Path Loss (FSPL)
The primary loss factor is the signal attenuation over distance in open air.
F SP L(dB) = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) − 147.55 (1)
Substituting our target parameters ( d = 5000m, f = 915 × 106Hz):
F SP L ≈ 105.66 dB
3.2 Link Margin Calculation
The expected received power ( Prx) is calculated by summing gains and subtracting losses:
Prx = Ptx + Gtx + Grx − F SP L (2)
Prx = 24 dBm + 2 dBi + 2 dBi − 105.66 dB
Prx ≈ −77.66 dBm
The Link Margin is the safety buffer between the received signal and the receiver
sensitivity (−110 dBm).
Margin = −77.66 dBm − (−110 dBm) ≈ +32.34 dB
A margin of +32.34 dB justifies that the hardware is capable of distances significantly
greater than 5km under ideal conditions [3].
4 Operational Challenges: The “Donut” Effect
Dipole antennas have significant nulls (blind spots) along their vertical axis. The radiation
pattern resembles a donut, with the antenna sitting in the hole [4].

The Risk: If the ground station antenna points vertically, and the rocket is overhead,
the “nulls” align, causing signal loss.

A Post-Document Summary & Recommendations
A.1 Current Setup Issues
Despite the calculated 32dB margin, real-world factors can degrade performance:
• The Nulls: As detailed in Section 4, aligning the tips of the antennas causes signal
dropout.
• Polarization Mismatch: The rocket antenna is vertically polarized. If the rocket
tilts or spins during descent, polarization mismatch can cause losses of 3dB to 20dB
[2].
• Wasted Energy: Omnidirectional antennas radiate energy into the ground and
behind the station, wasting power that could be directed at the rocket.
A.2 Recommendations for Ground Station Upgrade
To ensure a robust 5km link, we recommend upgrading the Ground Station antenna
while keeping the duck antenna on the rocket for aerodynamic efficiency.
Option 1: 900MHz Bi-Quad (Highly Recommended)
The Bi-Quad is often preferred by hobbyists over patch antennas because it is easier to
build with standard tools and less sensitive to material errors.
• Pros: Excellent gain ( ≈ 11 dBi), wide beamwidth ( ≈ 60◦), forgiving fabrication
tolerances.
• Reference: Changpuak Bi-Quad Calculator [6].
Figure 5: Example of a Homebrew 915MHz Bi-Quad Antenna.
Option 2: 900MHz Patch Antenna
The Patch Antenna is a strong candidate for this application due to its directional gain
and flat form factor.
• Pros: Good gain ( ≈ 7 dBi), robust flat form factor, easier to mount on a tripod
than a Yagi.
4
• Cons: Extremely sensitive to material variations (FR-4 fiberglass quality).
• Design Resource: See Phil’s Lab for detailed PCB patch design procedures [5].
A.3 Crucial Tooling
For any DIY antenna build (Patch or Bi-Quad), a NanoVNA is mandatory. Without
verifying the resonant frequency, the antenna may be tuned to the wrong frequency (e.g.,
880MHz), significantly reducing range.
